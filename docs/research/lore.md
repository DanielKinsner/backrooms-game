# The Backrooms — Deep Lore Research Brief

A research dossier for the game team. Goal: ground the design in what fans of the Backrooms actually care about so the adaptation lands as authentic instead of generic. Every claim here is sourced at the bottom.

---

## 1. The Origin: 4chan /x/, May 12 2019

On May 12, 2019, an anonymous user opened a thread on 4chan's paranormal board /x/ with the prompt "post disquieting images that just feel 'off'." The OP attached a photo of a yellow-walled, fluorescent-lit empty room — later traced (in May 2024, by a Backrooms Discord community digging through Wayback Machine archives) to a June 2002 renovation photo of the second floor of 807 Oregon Street, Oshkosh, Wisconsin: a former Rohner's Home Furnishings space being prepped for a HobbyTown franchise.

A reply to that thread is the seed of the entire phenomenon. The exact wording — which the game should treat as scripture — is:

> "If you're not careful and you noclip out of reality in the wrong areas, you'll end up in the Backrooms, where it's nothing but the stink of old moist carpet, the madness of mono-yellow, the endless background noise of fluorescent lights at maximum hum-buzz, and approximately six hundred million square miles of randomly segmented empty rooms to be trapped in. God save you if you hear something wandering around nearby, because it sure as hell has heard you..."

Key load-bearing phrases the fanbase recognizes instantly:

- **"noclip out of reality"** — the verb the community uses for accidental entry. Borrowed from video games (clipping through a level's geometry into the void). The Backrooms ARE the void you fall through.
- **"the stink of old moist carpet"** — smell is established first. This is sensory horror, not visual horror.
- **"the madness of mono-yellow"** — color as antagonist. Yellow is framed as itself a hostile force.
- **"hum-buzz"** of fluorescents "at maximum" — sound is constant, never relieved, migraine-inducing.
- **"approximately six hundred million square miles"** — scale figure. The space is roughly three times the surface area of Earth. Escape is statistical, not strategic.
- **"God save you if you hear something wandering nearby"** — the original text is famously ambiguous about whether there are entities at all. Something MAY be there. That ambiguity is the whole point.

That single reply is fewer than 80 words. The game should not invent contradictions to it; it should expand outward from it.

## 2. The Aesthetic Movement That Birthed It: Liminal Spaces and Kenopsia

The Backrooms didn't appear from nothing. It was the breakout star of the liminal-space aesthetic — empty malls, dim hotel hallways at 3 a.m., school corridors during summer, indoor pools after hours, McDonald's PlayPlaces with the lights half-off. The defining trait: a place built for crowds, photographed when empty.

The clinical-feeling term the community adopted is **kenopsia**, coined by John Koenig in *The Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows*: "the eerie, forlorn atmosphere of a place that's usually bustling with people but is now abandoned and quiet." Where liminality is about transition between states, kenopsia is about the residue of absent crowds. The Backrooms is pure kenopsia weaponized into infinity.

The psychology, drawn from environmental-psychology writing on the phenomenon, has three components fans intuitively feel:

1. **Uncanny-valley architecture.** A space is recognizable enough to read as "office back hallway" or "hotel pool deck," but the proportions, lighting, or context are subtly wrong. The brain's pattern-matcher fires "safe, familiar" and "threat, unknown" at the same time.
2. **Removed safety signals.** No warmth, no other humans, no smell of food, no daylight, no weather. The threat-detection system stays active because nothing tells it to stand down.
3. **Memory contamination.** These spaces resemble places we've all been (a hallway behind a department store, the corridor outside a hotel ballroom), so they feel like personal memories that have gone wrong. The horror is autobiographical.

Crucially: the liminal aesthetic exploded in popularity in March 2020 alongside COVID lockdowns. Empty real-world malls, empty offices, empty schools became everyone's daily visual diet. The Backrooms wasn't a fictional place anymore — it was an architectural mood that matched the world outside the window. Build the game knowing the fanbase associates this aesthetic with the specific loneliness of 2020-2022.

## 3. Canonical Level 0 — The Production Bible

Across the major wikis (Backrooms Wikidot, the Fandom Backrooms Wiki, Miraheze), Level 0 — variously nicknamed "The Lobby," "Threshold," or "The Yellow Hell" — is the consensus baseline. The game's opening hour should treat this list as a checklist:

- **Yellowed, slightly mildewed wallpaper.** Greyish-yellow base. The most-described pattern is a thin vertical line with a small arrow-like motif repeating between lines. The yellow is sickly, not warm.
- **Damp, moist carpet.** Old commercial-grade short-pile, water-stained in patches. The smell is part of the canon — "moist carpet" appears in the original text. If the game has any olfactory cue at all (visible mold, condensation, water rings), include it.
- **Fluorescent ceiling lighting.** Inconsistent placement (not a clean grid), some flickering, some out. The hum-buzz is constant, louder than real fluorescents, and produces migraines that persist after exiting. This sound layer must run continuously — it is the level's voice.
- **Sparse electrical outlets** on the walls, randomly placed and largely useless.
- **Non-Euclidean layout.** Walking in a straight line can return you to your starting point. Retracing steps yields different rooms than you came through. No two rooms have the same shape. There is no map that can be drawn. This is the single most important mechanical property — if the game has a coherent traversable floor plan, it isn't the Backrooms.
- **No windows, no exits, no signage, no furniture, no power outlets that do anything, no other humans.**
- **In the purist canon, no entities.** The original 4chan post says only "if you hear something." Purists hold that Level 0 has no monsters — the dread, the disorientation, the hum, the smell, the infinite are the antagonist. The DREAD is the entity. The game should respect this for a long stretch: hours of play with no creature visible, only suggestion, before anything is shown.

## 4. Expanded Lore Worth Borrowing Tastefully

The post-2019 community built a sprawling mythos. Most of it is fan-fiction sprawl, but a few elements are widely accepted enough that fans expect them. Borrow selectively.

- **Noclipping.** The verb of entry. Falling through reality, usually through a floor or wall that shouldn't give way. Often associated with corners of vision, dark hallways, or moments of inattention. Some lore says you must be alone.
- **Almond Water.** Sweet, almond-scented liquid found in containers (bottles, vending machines, jugs) scattered through the levels. Restores some sanity / staves off the mental degradation of prolonged exposure. Drinkable in-fiction, slightly off in flavor. A great UI-free healing item: you find it, you drink it, you feel better. It predates the Backrooms in obscure internet lore, which is part of its credibility.
- **Entities — to be used SPARINGLY:**
    - **Hounds** — bipedal-to-quadrupedal predators, run fast, hunt by sound, attack in packs. Survival rule: maintain a steady pace, don't sprint, don't stop.
    - **Smilers** — glowing teeth and eyes in the dark, otherwise invisible. Rule: do not look them in the eye.
    - **Skin-Stealers / Still Lifes** — humanoid imitations with wrong faces, duplicated fingers, or distorted proportions. Born from the Backrooms "copying" people.
    - **The Lifeform / "the unseen"** — Kane Pixels' signature creature. A hive-like hunter that mimics human cries for help using the throats of victims. Acute hearing, blind. Never shown clearly.
- **Levels beyond 0:**
    - **Level 1, "Habitable Zone"** — concrete warehouse, exposed rebar, fog at ankle height, dim wall-mounted fluorescents, scattered supply crates that materialize and vanish. Habitable indefinitely if careful. The first place that feels like it could have humans.
    - **Level 2, "Pipe Dreams" / "Abandoned Utility Halls"** — pipes, valves, gauges, dim red bulbs, narrow corridors. Reached by walking far in Level 1 until pipes "fade in."
- **The M.E.G. (Major Explorer Group)** — paramilitary/scientific organization of wanderers documenting the levels and running outposts. Fan-canon, but ubiquitous. A good way to deliver lore via found documents without needing live NPCs.

**Borrow taxonomy:** Use Level 0 as 70%+ of the game. Use Level 1 and Level 2 to vary pace and signal "the world is bigger than you thought." Use Almond Water and noclipping as universal grammar. Use entities only after long buildup, and use Hounds / Smilers / something Kane-Pixels-shaped — not all of them.

## 5. Kane Pixels and the Gold Standard

On January 7, 2022, then-16-year-old Kane Parsons (online as "Kane Pixels"), based in Northern California, posted "The Backrooms (Found Footage)" to YouTube. It went viral within days, racking up tens of millions of views and earning Parsons — at 18 — an A24 feature deal. The series follows the **A-Sync Research Institute** (alternatively styled "Async"), a fictional shadowy organization that accidentally discovers "The Complex" (their internal name for the Backrooms) in the late 1980s and tries to document it. The first video is framed as 1991 amateur-film footage: Kane the cameraman is shooting a B-movie, the director tells him to back up for a wide shot, he steps backward, phases through the floor, and falls into the Backrooms. The remaining nine minutes are him wandering and then being chased by something that throws a chair before catching him as the camera tumbles.

**Production reality:** entirely CGI in Blender + Adobe After Effects, with a VHS filter on top to disguise the rendering. One person, about a month for the first video.

**Why it is treated as the gold standard.** Six reinforcing reasons fans cite:

1. **Restraint.** The creature is barely visible, ever. Critics (Kotaku, academics) singled this out: the Backrooms is scary "because it invites you to interpret what's not shown." The longer a monster is on screen, the less it scares. Kane shows it for less than two seconds across nine minutes.
2. **1990s VHS aesthetic.** Grainy compression artifacts, chromatic aberration, blown-out highlights, mono audio, date-stamp burn-in, tracking glitches. This is **analog horror** in the lineage of Kris Straub's *Local 58* (2015, considered the origin of the genre and the source of the name) and Alex Kister's *The Mandela Catalogue*. The format itself codes the footage as "real and old," making the brain process it as document instead of fiction.
3. **Nostalgia weaponized.** Parsons has described his work as "a manifestation of a poorly remembered recollection of the late 1990s and early 2000s." The format isn't a gimmick; it's a memory trigger that primes the viewer to feel they have been there before.
4. **Plausible camera operator.** A real person holding a real camera moves in specific ways — out of breath, looking the wrong way, leaving the shutter open too long, fumbling. Kane's camerawork mimics this exactly, which is much harder than it looks and is the single biggest "tell" that separates Kane-tier work from cheap imitations.
5. **Diegetic sound design.** No score. Just camera mic hiss, footsteps on damp carpet, the hum, distant impacts, and breathing. When something musical happens it is wrong.
6. **Worldbuilding via implication.** The A-Sync logo, employee badges, briefcase contents, lab footage, audio cassettes — these are dropped without explanation across the series. Fans assemble the lore. The story is never narrated.

For the game: any moment where the player can hear themselves breathe, hear their footfalls change as the carpet gets wetter, and hear the fluorescent buzz hitch slightly when something nearby has moved — that's Kane-tier. Any moment with swelling orchestral horror strings or a clear monster reveal is not.

## 6. Why It Resonates — The Cultural and Psychological Why

The Backrooms exploded in popularity 2020-2022. That timing is not a coincidence.

- **Pandemic-emptied spaces became real.** Lockdowns made empty malls, schools, offices, gyms, and airports a shared global image. The Backrooms aesthetic was simply daily life with the lights left on. The fiction confirmed what the world looked like.
- **Generational liminality.** Millennials and Gen Z were already living through a structural liminality — economic precarity, the death of the mall-era third place, the shift from physical to digital "rooms," the suspended feeling of remote work and remote school. The Backrooms is that mood given geometry.
- **The death of the mall.** Empty-mall and dead-arcade liminal imagery is a specific generational grief — the disappearance of the 1990s-2000s indoor public square. Backrooms aesthetics borrow heavily from this. Wallpaper, carpet, fluorescents: it is the texture of childhood third spaces, abandoned.
- **Threat without referent.** In an era of free-floating anxiety (climate, politics, algorithmic feeds), a horror about something you cannot identify, in a space you cannot map, with no clear exit, no clear enemy, and no clear rules — feels true. The Backrooms doesn't symbolize a specific fear; it symbolizes the shape of modern fear.
- **Participatory mythos.** Like SCP before it, the Backrooms is a wiki, not a book. Fans contribute levels, entities, and groups. Ownership is distributed. This makes adaptations a delicate business — fans aren't passive consumers, they are co-authors, and they can tell when a property has been "taken" from them.

## 7. What a Game Adaptation Must Get Right

To feel authentic to the fanbase, the game must commit to:

1. **Restraint as a design principle.** Long stretches of nothing. Hours where the player questions whether anything is in here at all. The dread does the work.
2. **Sensory completeness.** Yellow that reads as sickly, not cheerful. Carpet that visibly absorbs the player's footfall sound. A fluorescent hum that never stops, with subtle modulation when something is near. Implied smell (visible damp, condensation, water rings).
3. **True non-Euclidean traversal.** Spaces that fold, loop, and rearrange off-camera. No mappable floor plan. Doors that lead back into rooms you just left. The map screen should be useless, or absent.
4. **Found-footage / VHS visual grammar (at least optional).** Chromatic aberration, mild compression, head-switching glitches, a date stamp. Treat it as an aesthetic register the player can toggle into during cutscenes or "tape" sequences. Diegetic camera if possible.
5. **Diegetic sound.** No music score on Level 0. Footfalls, breath, hum, distant impacts. Music can arrive later, broken, in deeper levels.
6. **Originalist Level 0 first.** Don't open in a creature corridor. Open in the yellow, alone, lost, for a long time. Earn the first entity.
7. **Lore by artifact, not by narrator.** Found documents, half-erased whiteboards, A-Sync-style badges, audio cassettes. Let the player assemble.
8. **Almond Water and noclipping as universal grammar.** Use these terms. Fans will hear them and lean in.
9. **Procedural / probabilistic geometry.** The fanbase expects to feel that the space generated itself around them. If two players' runs feel identical, immersion breaks.
10. **Respect the "God save you if you hear something" ambiguity.** Sometimes nothing is there. Sometimes the sound was the building. The player should never be sure.

## 8. The Top 5 Mistakes That Make Backrooms Adaptations Feel Cheap

These are the failure modes critics and fans have hammered on repeatedly across the existing game adaptations (*The Backrooms 1998*, various Unity asset-flips) and even in the more mixed reactions to the 2026 A24 film:

1. **Cheap, frequent jumpscares.** *The Backrooms 1998* was specifically panned for loud, purposeless jumpscares that "blow out your eardrums" and ruin the atmosphere. The Backrooms is dread, not startle. Every jumpscare burns dread capital and gives back nothing.
2. **Showing the monster too much, too clearly, too early.** Even the A24 film took criticism here: "the longer you show the monster, the less scary and interesting it becomes." Kane Pixels' restraint is the bar.
3. **Coherent, mappable, finite levels.** The moment the player realizes the level is just a maze on a grid that they can solve, the Backrooms collapses into a regular horror corridor. Non-Euclidean geometry has to be functional, not cosmetic.
4. **Generic "creepy" lighting and color.** Going too dark, too desaturated, too purple-and-teal "horror palette." The Backrooms is specifically over-lit, mono-yellow, and flat. Eye-strain bright, not shadow-bright. Many adaptations chicken out of how aggressively yellow it should be because yellow does not "read horror" — that misreading is the failure.
5. **Stuffing in fan-wiki creature zoo too fast.** Dumping Smilers, Hounds, Skin-Stealers, Partygoers, Clumps, and Bacteria in the first hour signals to fans that the developers grabbed the wiki, not the feeling. Fans want the *original creepypasta's atmosphere* extended for hours before any creature cameo. Treat the entity roster as dessert, not the meal.

A sixth honorable-mention failure: **scoring it like a horror movie.** Orchestral stings, droning synths laid over every corridor — the Backrooms wants diegetic-only. Music is permission for the player to know they are in a game. Withholding music is one of the cheapest, highest-impact authenticity moves available.
